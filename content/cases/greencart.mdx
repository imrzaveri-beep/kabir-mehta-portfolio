## Problem

A full basket was not translating into completed orders. The fictional GreenCart team saw shoppers hesitate when delivery timing, fees and substitutions appeared late in checkout. The interface treated a weekly shop like an impulse purchase.

The goal was to let households make a clear commitment, with the information that mattered visible before the final step.

## Research

For this illustrative case study, I mapped the shopping journey from a saved list to a delivered order. The research plan combined checkout observation, support-theme review and short interviews about the weekly routine.

The working hypotheses were uncertainty about delivery slots, surprise costs and a loss of context when editing a basket. These are design assumptions for the demo, not claims about a real user study.

## Ideation

Three directions tested different priorities: a fastest-path checkout, a detailed order summary and a household-first basket. I chose the last approach, keeping delivery details close to the products and making edits reversible.

A quick comparison matrix helped the team separate a simpler appearance from a genuinely simpler task.

## Design

The concept makes the selected delivery window and full total visible early. Quantity controls keep their state when an item is updated. Substitution preferences are expressed in plain language, and a review step leaves room to catch mistakes.

The original desktop and mobile concepts focus on easy scanning, generous targets and clear product information. They are static design illustrations, not a shopping or payment service.

## Results

The supplied fictional outcome is **22% less checkout drop-off**. A real assessment would specify the baseline, time period and eligible sessions before attributing the change to design.

The supporting measurement plan tracks basket edits, slot availability failures and support requests. A completed order is only a win if the customer receives what they expected.

## Key learnings

- Make commitments visible before asking for a decision.
- Preserve context when customers edit.
- Treat substitutions as a choice, not a footnote.
- Pair conversion metrics with delivery quality.
